## Why check-calls are an operating system

Check-calls are not a polite ritual. They are how a dry-van desk discovers reality before the broker, the shipper, or the detention clock does. A missed appointment usually starts as a quiet problem hours earlier: traffic, a slow shipper, a driver who is tired, a yard that will not open until morning.

On a US long-haul desk covering Pacific hours from India overnight, check-calls are also how handoffs stay honest. The morning board should not be a scavenger hunt.

## Cadence that works under load

Use a rhythm tied to the load, not to a generic hourly ping:

1. **Post-dispatch confirmation** — driver has rate con notes, appointment windows, special instructions, and a clear empty/next plan.
2. **Pre-pickup window** — enough lead time to re-power if the truck will not make it.
3. **At pickup / rolling** — confirm loaded, seal if required, actual weight if available, and first ETA to delivery.
4. **Mid-transit** — especially on multi-day hauls; catch HOS and weather before they corner you.
5. **Pre-delivery** — confirm appointment, lumper/detention risk, and receiver quirks.
6. **Delivered + paperwork** — POD path, OS&D notes, and next empty time.

When volume is high, prioritize: trucks inside appointment risk windows, new drivers, lanes with chronic detention, and any load the broker has already asked about.

## What to ask (without wasting the driver’s time)

Keep questions short and concrete:

- Where are you, and are you still on the planned route?
- Current HOS remaining (drive / on-duty) in plain language.
- Will you make the appointment as planned? If not, what is the realistic ETA?
- Any shipper/receiver delays, lumper issues, or equipment concerns?
- Any accessorials we should start documenting now?

Then **log the answer** in the tracker the team actually uses. A check-call that lives only in chat is a check-call that disappears at shift change.

## Escalation without panic

Escalate early when:

- The revised ETA will miss the appointment window.
- Detention hours are accumulating and the broker has not been notified.
- The driver reports unsafe pressure to break HOS.
- The load needs a re-power decision before the market window closes.

Tone matters. Drivers should hear calm ownership: “Thanks — I’m updating the broker with this ETA and I’ll come back with options.” Brokers should hear facts and a plan, not excuses.

## Handoff notes for overnight / multi-dispatcher desks

Write handoffs as if the next person has zero context:

- Truck / driver / load ID
- Last known location + time
- Appointment commitments
- Open risks (detention, weather, HOS, broker attitude)
- Next action already promised to someone

## Takeaway

Check-call discipline is how a remote desk earns trust. Brokers remember the dispatcher who called problems in early. Drivers remember the desk that did not leave them alone at a dark warehouse. Reliability is built in these small, repeated conversations.
