## Pretty freight vs. workable freight

A high RPM on the board can still be a bad load. If the empty is wrong, the appointment is fantasy, or the driver’s hours cannot legally finish the pull, you have bought a problem. Good load planning starts with the truck’s real state: location, hours, preferred home time, and the next profitable headhaul — not with the loudest post on DAT.

## The planning checklist

Before you book:

1. **Empty time and place** — confirmed, not assumed.
2. **Deadhead vs. revenue miles** — know the break-even for that truck that week.
3. **Appointment realism** — live traffic and typical dwell for that shipper/receiver.
4. **HOS fit** — drive and on-duty remaining, reset needs, and sleeper strategy if relevant.
5. **After-delivery options** — do not strand the truck in a dead market without a plan B.
6. **Broker / shipper risk** — known detention offenders, lumper surprises, or paperwork friction.

## Lane strategy for PNW & Southwest focus

A concentrated lane map (Washington, Oregon, California, Arizona, Utah, Colorado and related corridors) reduces surprise. You learn which yards run late, which mountain passes slow winter freight, and which backhauls actually pay. Specialization also makes broker conversations sharper: you are not “any van” — you are capacity that understands those lanes.

## When to take a weaker rate

Sometimes a short or softer-paying load is correct:

- Positions the truck for a stronger outbound.
- Protects a driver’s home-time commitment.
- Avoids a multi-day sit that costs more than the RPM gap.
- Repairs a relationship after an earlier service miss (used sparingly, with eyes open).

Document *why* you took it so the desk does not invent a new “we always take cheap freight” habit.

## When to decline

Say no when:

- Hours cannot finish safely and legally.
- Detention risk is extreme and the rate does not price it in.
- The broker cannot confirm appointment or commodity details you need.
- The load requires equipment or service you do not run.

A clean “no” with a reason preserves the relationship for the next good truck.

## Takeaway

Load planning is risk management with a P&L. The desk that respects HOS and empty truth will outlast the desk that only chases the highest posted rate.
